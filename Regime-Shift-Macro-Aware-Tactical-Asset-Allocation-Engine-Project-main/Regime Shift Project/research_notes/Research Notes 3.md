# Notebook 03 — HMM Theory

# Chapter 1: Research Objective and Modeling Philosophy

---

## Research Objective

The primary objective of Notebook 03 was to develop a statistically rigorous and economically interpretable Hidden Markov Model (HMM) capable of identifying latent market regimes for portfolio allocation and risk management.

Rather than maximizing predictive accuracy alone, the model was designed to satisfy three objectives simultaneously:

- Statistical validity
- Financial interpretability
- Practical applicability

The HMM will later serve as the regime detection engine for the portfolio optimization framework developed in subsequent notebooks.

---

## Initial Research Questions

Before selecting a model, the following research questions were identified:

- Can latent market regimes be inferred from observable financial variables?
- How many hidden regimes are economically meaningful?
- Which probability distributions best describe market observations?
- Which covariance structure best captures changing market relationships?
- How should regime uncertainty be incorporated into portfolio allocation?
- How can overfitting be minimized while maintaining economic interpretability?

These questions guided every modeling decision throughout Notebook 03.

---

## Modeling Philosophy

A fundamental design principle of this project is that financial markets are probabilistic rather than deterministic.

Instead of assigning each observation to a single market state with certainty, the model should estimate the probability of multiple competing regimes.

This philosophy motivated the use of Hidden Markov Models instead of deterministic classification approaches.

---

## Why Hidden Markov Models?

Several approaches for market regime detection were considered conceptually.

Examples include

- Rule-based market classification
- Clustering methods
- Gaussian Mixture Models
- Hidden Markov Models

The Hidden Markov Model was selected because it uniquely combines

- probabilistic inference,
- temporal dependence,
- latent state estimation,
- sequential modeling.

Unlike static clustering methods, HMMs explicitly model transitions between market regimes through time.

---

## Research Assumptions

The following assumptions were adopted for the remainder of the project:

- Market regimes are latent variables.
- Observable financial variables are generated by these hidden regimes.
- Regime evolution follows the first-order Markov property.
- Observations are conditionally independent given the hidden state.
- Financial markets exhibit uncertainty that should be modeled probabilistically rather than deterministically.

These assumptions establish the theoretical foundation for all subsequent implementation decisions.

---

## Expected Project Outcome

At the completion of this project, the HMM should be capable of

- identifying economically meaningful market regimes,
- estimating regime probabilities,
- supporting regime-aware portfolio allocation,
- improving risk management,
- providing interpretable market state classifications.

The model will ultimately be evaluated using statistical diagnostics, economic interpretation, and out-of-sample portfolio performance rather than likelihood alone.

---

## Key Research Decision

**Decision**

Adopt a probabilistic Hidden Markov Model as the core market regime detection framework.

**Reasoning**

The HMM provides the best balance between

- statistical rigor,
- temporal modeling,
- uncertainty quantification,
- financial interpretability,
- integration with portfolio optimization.

# Chapter 2: Hidden Markov Model Formulation

---

## Research Objective

The objective of this stage was to formulate a probabilistic model capable of representing market dynamics through latent (unobservable) market regimes while allowing observable financial variables to be generated from those regimes.

The formulation needed to satisfy both statistical rigor and financial realism.

---

## Research Questions

During model formulation, the following questions were considered:

- What should represent the hidden states?
- What variables should be treated as observations?
- How should regimes evolve through time?
- How should observations be generated?
- Which assumptions are reasonable for financial markets?

---

## Hidden State Definition

The hidden states were defined as latent market regimes.

These regimes are not directly observable but are assumed to generate the observable market characteristics.

Typical interpretations include:

- Bull Market
- Bear Market
- Crisis Market

The exact labels are assigned only after model estimation based on the statistical characteristics of each inferred state.

---

## Observable Variables

The HMM was designed to operate on observable market features rather than raw prices.

Candidate features considered include:

- Daily returns
- Realized volatility
- VIX
- Momentum
- Rolling correlations
- Bond returns

These variables collectively provide information about the underlying market environment.

The final feature set will be finalized during the feature engineering and implementation stages.

---

## State Evolution Assumption

A first-order Markov assumption was adopted.

This assumes that the probability of tomorrow's regime depends only on today's regime.

Mathematically,

$$
P(Z_{t+1}|Z_t,Z_{t-1},\ldots)
=
P(Z_{t+1}|Z_t)
$$

This assumption provides a balance between model flexibility and computational tractability.

---

## Observation Assumption

Conditional independence was assumed.

Once the hidden regime is known,

the current observation is assumed to be independent of previous observations.

This simplifies inference while remaining consistent with the standard Hidden Markov Model framework.

---

## Emission Distribution

Several possible emission distributions were considered conceptually.

Examples include:

- Gaussian
- Student's t
- Mixture distributions
- Non-parametric approaches

For the initial implementation, Gaussian emissions were selected.

---

## Decision

**Use multivariate Gaussian emission distributions.**

### Rationale

Reasons for this decision include:

- Widely used in financial HMM literature.
- Computationally efficient.
- Supported by mature software libraries.
- Sufficiently flexible when combined with multiple hidden regimes.
- Provides interpretable mean vectors and covariance matrices.

Alternative distributions may be explored as future extensions.

---

## Model Parameters

The complete Hidden Markov Model was formulated using four parameter groups:

- Initial state probabilities
- Transition probability matrix
- Mean vector for each regime
- Covariance matrix for each regime

These parameters completely specify the probabilistic model and will later be estimated using the Baum–Welch algorithm.

---

## Expected Outputs

The completed HMM should ultimately provide:

- Estimated hidden market regimes.
- Transition probabilities between regimes.
- Regime-specific statistical characteristics.
- Daily posterior regime probabilities.
- Most probable historical state sequence.

These outputs form the foundation for subsequent portfolio construction.

---

## Research Decision Summary

| Component | Decision | Reason |
|-----------|----------|--------|
| Hidden States | Latent market regimes | Markets are not directly observable. |
| State Dynamics | First-order Markov chain | Simple, interpretable, computationally efficient. |
| Observation Model | Conditional independence | Standard HMM assumption enabling efficient inference. |
| Emission Distribution | Multivariate Gaussian | Financial interpretability and computational tractability. |
| Observations | Financial features | Capture different dimensions of market behavior. |

---

## Future Considerations

Potential future improvements include:

- Heavy-tailed emission distributions.
- Time-varying transition probabilities.
- Hidden semi-Markov models.
- Bayesian Hidden Markov Models.
- Regime-switching stochastic volatility models.

These extensions were intentionally excluded from the current project to maintain a balance between theoretical rigor, implementation complexity, and practical applicability.

# Chapter 3: Inference Strategy and Parameter Estimation

---

## Research Objective

Once the Hidden Markov Model was formulated, the next objective was to determine how latent market regimes and model parameters could be estimated efficiently.

The chosen inference methodology needed to be

- computationally tractable,
- statistically rigorous,
- scalable to long financial time series,
- suitable for probabilistic decision making.

---

## Research Questions

The following questions guided this stage of the research:

- How can the probability of the observed market data be computed efficiently?
- How can hidden market regimes be inferred?
- How can model parameters be estimated when the regimes are unknown?
- How should uncertainty be incorporated during estimation?

---

## Computational Challenge

A direct evaluation of all possible hidden-state sequences was rejected because of its exponential computational complexity.

For

- \(K\) hidden states
- \(T\) observations

the total number of possible hidden paths is

$$
K^T.
$$

For realistic financial datasets containing thousands of observations, exhaustive enumeration becomes computationally infeasible.

Dynamic programming algorithms were therefore adopted.

---

## Inference Algorithms Adopted

The following algorithms were selected for the project.

| Algorithm | Purpose |
|------------|---------|
| Forward Algorithm | Compute the likelihood of the observed data |
| Backward Algorithm | Compute future conditional probabilities |
| Forward–Backward | Estimate posterior regime probabilities |
| Viterbi Algorithm | Infer the most probable regime sequence |
| Baum–Welch (EM) | Estimate model parameters |

Each algorithm addresses a different inference problem within the Hidden Markov Model framework.

---

## Parameter Estimation Strategy

The Baum–Welch algorithm was selected as the parameter estimation method.

Reasons include:

- Standard estimation procedure for HMMs.
- Iteratively improves model likelihood.
- Naturally estimates transition probabilities, means, and covariance matrices.
- Produces statistically consistent parameter estimates under the HMM assumptions.

The algorithm iteratively alternates between

- Expectation (E-step)
- Maximization (M-step)

until convergence.

---

## Soft Probability Estimation

A key design decision was to retain posterior regime probabilities instead of assigning observations directly to a single regime during training.

Reasons include:

- Financial markets rarely exist in one pure regime.
- Soft probabilities preserve uncertainty.
- More stable parameter estimation.
- Reduced sensitivity to classification errors.

This aligns with the probabilistic nature of financial markets.

---

## Hard Regime Estimation

Although soft probabilities are retained during estimation,

hard regime labels remain useful for

- visualization,
- interpretation,
- historical analysis,
- performance attribution.

The Viterbi algorithm was therefore retained as a complementary inference tool.

---

## Hybrid Inference Strategy

The project adopts a hybrid inference framework.

### Soft Regimes

Used for

- portfolio allocation,
- risk management,
- probability-based decision making.

### Hard Regimes

Used for

- regime visualization,
- historical interpretation,
- reporting,
- communication.

This approach balances statistical efficiency with interpretability.

---

## Convergence Criterion

Model training terminates when improvements in log-likelihood become negligible.

This indicates that the parameter estimates have stabilized.

Convergence alone, however, is not treated as evidence of model quality.

Additional statistical and financial validation procedures are required before accepting the model.

---

## Research Decision Summary

| Component | Decision | Reason |
|-----------|----------|--------|
| Likelihood Computation | Forward Algorithm | Efficient dynamic programming solution. |
| Posterior Estimation | Forward–Backward | Preserves uncertainty. |
| State Decoding | Viterbi Algorithm | Provides interpretable regime sequence. |
| Parameter Estimation | Baum–Welch (EM) | Standard HMM learning algorithm. |
| Training Labels | Soft probabilities | More statistically efficient than hard assignments. |
| Final Interpretation | Hybrid soft + hard regimes | Combines portfolio optimization with interpretability. |

---

## Future Considerations

Potential extensions include:

- Bayesian estimation techniques.
- Variational inference.
- Online Expectation–Maximization.
- Sequential parameter updating.
- Particle filtering for nonlinear state estimation.

These methods were excluded to maintain consistency with the Gaussian Hidden Markov Model framework adopted in this project.

# Chapter 4: Model Specification and Selection

---

## Research Objective

The objective of this stage was to determine the most appropriate Hidden Markov Model specification for market regime detection.

Several competing model configurations were evaluated with the goal of balancing

- statistical performance,
- economic interpretability,
- robustness,
- computational efficiency.

Rather than selecting the model with the highest likelihood alone, both statistical and financial considerations were incorporated into the final decision.

---

## Research Questions

The following questions guided the model selection process:

- How many hidden states should be used?
- Which covariance structure best represents financial markets?
- How should competing models be compared?
- How can overfitting be minimized?
- Which model is most useful for portfolio management?

---

# Number of Hidden States

Several model specifications were considered conceptually.

### Candidate Models

- 2-State HMM
- 3-State HMM
- 4-State HMM

---

## Evaluation

### Two-State Model

Advantages

- Simple.
- Computationally efficient.
- Easy to interpret.

Limitations

- Oversimplifies market dynamics.
- Bear and Crisis periods are frequently merged into a single regime.
- Provides limited information for risk management.

---

### Three-State Model

Advantages

- Naturally separates

    - Bull
    - Bear
    - Crisis

- Economically interpretable.
- Appropriate balance between complexity and robustness.
- Stable across different market environments.

Limitations

- May not capture very fine market transitions.

---

### Four-State Model

Advantages

- Greater flexibility.
- Higher in-sample likelihood.

Limitations

- Increased parameter complexity.
- Greater overfitting risk.
- Regime interpretation becomes less stable.
- Additional regime often lacks clear economic meaning.

---

## Final Decision

**Three hidden states were selected.**

### Rationale

The three-state specification provides

- meaningful economic interpretation,
- sufficient model flexibility,
- improved robustness,
- practical usefulness for portfolio construction.

Although four-state models may achieve higher in-sample likelihood, they introduce additional complexity without consistently improving out-of-sample performance.

---

# Covariance Structure

Several covariance structures were evaluated conceptually.

### Candidate Structures

- Full
- Diagonal
- Tied
- Spherical

---

## Evaluation

### Full Covariance

Advantages

- Models changing correlations.
- Captures dependence between financial variables.
- Best represents market dynamics.

Limitations

- Larger number of parameters.
- Higher computational cost.

---

### Diagonal Covariance

Advantages

- Fast estimation.
- Reduced overfitting risk.

Limitations

- Assumes conditional independence.
- Ignores cross-asset relationships.

---

### Tied Covariance

Advantages

- Lower parameter count.
- Stable estimation.

Limitations

- Assumes identical covariance structure across all regimes.
- Unrealistic for financial markets.

---

### Spherical Covariance

Advantages

- Simplest specification.

Limitations

- Assumes equal variance in every direction.
- Financially unrealistic.

---

## Final Decision

**Full covariance was selected.**

### Rationale

Financial markets are characterized by

- changing correlations,
- volatility clustering,
- flight-to-quality,
- evolving dependence structures.

These characteristics are best represented by Full covariance despite the increased parameter complexity.

---

# Model Selection Criteria

Several statistical metrics were considered.

### Log-Likelihood

Useful for measuring statistical fit.

However,

higher likelihood alone was not considered sufficient because more complex models naturally achieve better in-sample fit.

---

### Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)

Balances

- model fit,
- parameter complexity.

Useful for comparing competing specifications.

---

### Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)

Applies a stronger penalty for model complexity.

Particularly useful when preventing overfitting.

---

## Final Decision

Model selection was based on a combination of

- Log-Likelihood,
- AIC,
- BIC,
- economic interpretation,
- robustness.

No single metric determined the final model.

---

# Model Interpretation

Statistical performance alone was considered insufficient.

The inferred regimes were also evaluated according to

- mean returns,
- volatility,
- VIX behavior,
- transition probabilities,
- expected regime duration,
- correspondence with historical market events.

Only economically meaningful regimes were considered acceptable.

---

# Final Model Specification

The final specification adopted for implementation is

| Component | Selected Specification |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Hidden States | 3 |
| Emission Distribution | Multivariate Gaussian |
| Covariance Structure | Full |
| Training Algorithm | Baum–Welch (EM) |
| Decoding Algorithm | Viterbi |
| Posterior Inference | Forward–Backward |

---

# Remaining Risks

Despite careful model selection, several limitations remain.

- Financial markets are non-stationary.
- Hidden state interpretation may evolve over time.
- Parameter estimates depend on feature quality.
- Local optima remain possible.
- Covariance estimation may become unstable for small datasets.

These risks motivate the validation procedures developed in the following chapter.

---

| Emission Distribution | Gaussian | Standard, interpretable, computationally efficient |
| Covariance | Full | Captures changing financial relationships |

# Chapter 5: Model Validation and Robustness Strategy

---

## Research Objective

After selecting the final Hidden Markov Model specification, the next objective was to establish a validation framework capable of assessing whether the model is statistically reliable, economically meaningful, and suitable for practical portfolio management.

The validation process was designed to evaluate both model performance and model robustness under changing market conditions.

---

## Research Questions

The following questions guided the validation stage:

- Has the model converged successfully?
- Are the inferred regimes economically meaningful?
- Does the model generalize beyond the training data?
- Is the model robust to different training configurations?
- Can the inferred regimes support practical investment decisions?

---

# Validation Philosophy

Model validation was approached from three complementary perspectives.

1. Statistical Validation
2. Economic Validation
3. Investment Validation

A model was considered suitable only if it satisfied all three criteria.

---

# Statistical Validation

The statistical evaluation focused on determining whether the optimization process produced a stable and reliable model.

The following diagnostics were considered:

- Log-Likelihood convergence.
- Convergence of the Baum–Welch algorithm.
- Model comparison using AIC.
- Model comparison using BIC.
- Stability across different random initializations.

These metrics assess the statistical quality of the estimated model but do not guarantee financial usefulness.

---

# Economic Validation

Statistical performance alone was considered insufficient.

The inferred hidden regimes were evaluated according to their economic interpretation.

The following characteristics were examined:

- Mean return of each regime.
- Volatility of each regime.
- VIX behavior.
- Transition probabilities.
- Expected regime duration.
- Alignment with major historical market events.

The objective was to ensure that each inferred state corresponded to a recognizable market environment.

---

# Investment Validation

The final stage of validation focused on practical usability.

The primary research question was:

> Can regime information improve portfolio construction?

Performance evaluation will be conducted in later notebooks using metrics such as:

- Risk-adjusted return.
- Sharpe ratio.
- Maximum drawdown.
- Portfolio turnover.
- Portfolio stability.

Investment performance was treated as the ultimate validation criterion.

---

# Walk-Forward Validation

A chronological validation framework was adopted to prevent look-ahead bias.

Instead of training the model once using the entire dataset,

the model will be retrained sequentially using historical information that would have been available at each point in time.

This approach better reflects real-world investment conditions.

---

# Robustness Strategy

Robustness was considered equally important as predictive performance.

The following robustness checks were incorporated into the research methodology:

- Multiple random initializations.
- Alternative training windows.
- Different feature combinations.
- Comparison of covariance structures.
- Comparison of different numbers of hidden states.
- Out-of-sample evaluation.

The objective is to identify models that remain stable under reasonable variations in model specification.

---

# Prevention of Common Modeling Errors

The following risks were explicitly considered during model development.

### Overfitting

Mitigation:

- AIC.
- BIC.
- Walk-forward validation.
- Preference for simpler interpretable models.

---

### Look-Ahead Bias

Mitigation:

- Strict chronological data splitting.
- Walk-forward validation.
- No future information during training.

---

### Data Leakage

Mitigation:

- Feature engineering performed using only historical information.
- Training statistics computed only from training data.

---

### Regime Thrashing

Mitigation:

- Preference for economically persistent regimes.
- Evaluation of transition probabilities.
- Expected regime duration analysis.

---

# Validation Checklist

Before accepting any HMM specification, the following questions should be answered.

- Has the EM algorithm converged?
- Is the log-likelihood stable?
- Are AIC and BIC competitive?
- Are the inferred regimes economically meaningful?
- Are transition probabilities realistic?
- Are regime durations plausible?
- Does the model perform consistently across different training windows?
- Does walk-forward testing support the model?
- Can the inferred regimes improve portfolio performance?

Only models satisfying these criteria will be considered suitable for implementation.

---

# Final Validation Strategy

The project adopts a layered validation framework.

| Validation Layer | Objective |
|------------------|-----------|
| Statistical Validation | Ensure numerical and statistical reliability |
| Economic Validation | Ensure meaningful market interpretation |
| Investment Validation | Evaluate portfolio usefulness |

This framework reduces the likelihood of selecting models that fit historical data well but fail in practical investment settings.

---

# Research Decision Summary

| Decision | Final Choice | Reason |
|-----------|--------------|--------|
| Validation Approach | Three-layer validation | Combines statistical and financial evaluation |
| Out-of-Sample Testing | Walk-forward validation | Prevents look-ahead bias |
| Model Comparison | Log-Likelihood + AIC + BIC | Balance fit and complexity |
| Robustness Testing | Multiple model configurations | Reduce overfitting risk |
| Final Acceptance | Portfolio performance and economic interpretation | Practical investment relevance |
